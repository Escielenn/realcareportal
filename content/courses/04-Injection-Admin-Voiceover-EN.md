Real Care, Inc. — Injection Administration Training
Voiceover Script (English)

Estimated Read Time: ~7 minutes at natural speaking pace

Welcome to Real Care! This training covers Injection Administration under Delegated Medication Administration. By the end of this training, you will be able to explain why injections are used, understand nurse delegation requirements, identify the supplies needed, demonstrate proper injection technique, follow infection control practices, recognize adverse reactions, safely dispose of sharps, and document medication administration correctly. Please note that caregivers and providers will also need to demonstrate competency during nurse observation.

Let's begin with the nurse delegation overview. Injection administration may only be performed when three conditions are met. First, a licensed Real Care, Inc. nurse must delegate the task to you. Second, you must receive training and demonstration. And third, you must complete competency verification. Delegation includes written instructions, protocol guidance, competency validation, and ongoing supervision. This process follows several regulations, including the Colorado Nurse Practice Act, Colorado Revised Statutes Section 12-255-131 on Delegation of Nursing Tasks, Colorado Board of Nursing Rule 3 CCR 716-1.13, HCPF IDD Waiver requirements, CDPHE infection control guidance, and Real Care agency policies and procedures.

Now let's talk about why injections are used. Medications are given by injection to deliver medication quickly into the body, provide medications that cannot be taken by mouth, manage chronic conditions such as diabetes or hormone therapy, and maintain consistent medication levels in the bloodstream. Proper technique is critical because it prevents infection, tissue damage, medication errors, and individual discomfort.

There are two types of injections you may administer. Subcutaneous injections, also called SubQ, deliver medication into the fat layer under the skin. Common medications given this way include insulin and some hormone medications. Typical injection sites are the abdomen, upper arm, and thigh. Intramuscular injections, also called IM, deliver medication into muscle tissue. Muscles absorb medication faster than fat tissue. Common sites for intramuscular injections include the deltoid or upper arm, the thigh, and the hip.

Before every injection, you must verify the Seven Rights of Medication Administration. These are: the Right Person — make sure you are giving the medication to the correct individual. The Right Medication — verify the medication matches the order. The Right Dose — confirm the amount is correct. The Right Route — ensure it is being given subcutaneously or intramuscularly as ordered. The Right Time — administer at the correct time. The Right Documentation — record everything accurately. And the Right Reason — understand why the medication is being given. Failure to verify these rights can result in serious medication errors.

There are situations when you should not give an injection. Do not administer medication if the medication appears contaminated, if the medication is expired, if the individual has a known allergy, if the injection site is red, swollen, bruised, or infected, if the medication order is unclear, or if the syringe or needle is damaged. In any of these situations, contact the prescriber, pharmacy, or nurse.

Before beginning, gather all required supplies. You will need the prescribed medication, a syringe and needle if not using a prefilled syringe, alcohol swabs, gloves, a sharps container, the Medication Administration Record or MAR, and a clean preparation surface. Never begin the procedure without all required supplies.

Infection control is essential and protects both you and the individual. Always wash your hands before and after the procedure, wear gloves, use sterile needles and syringes, clean the injection site with alcohol, and never reuse needles or syringes. Improper infection control can cause bloodborne infections, skin infections, and other serious complications.

Now let's walk through the preparation steps. To prepare the medication: first, perform hand hygiene. Second, put on gloves. Third, verify the medication order with the MAR. Fourth, check the medication label and expiration date. And fifth, prepare the medication according to nurse instructions. Never prepare medication if you are unsure about the instructions. To prepare the individual: first, explain the procedure to them. Second, ensure their privacy and dignity. Third, position them comfortably. And fourth, select the correct injection site. Some individuals prefer being informed before the injection, sitting or lying down, or using distraction techniques. Respect their preferences.

For the injection itself, follow these steps. First, clean the injection site with an alcohol swab. Second, allow the site to dry completely. Third, position the needle according to the injection type. Fourth, insert the needle at the proper angle. Fifth, inject the medication slowly. Sixth, remove the needle carefully. And seventh, apply pressure or a bandage if needed.

Immediately after the injection, dispose of the needle in the sharps container, remove your gloves, wash your hands, observe the individual for reactions, and provide reassurance and comfort. Never place used needles in regular trash.

Safe sharps disposal is critical. Always dispose of needles immediately, use an approved sharps container, never recap needles, never bend or break needles, and never leave sharps unattended. Improper sharps disposal can cause needlestick injuries and bloodborne pathogen exposure.

You need to know the difference between normal and concerning reactions. Normal reactions may include mild soreness and minor redness at the injection site. However, report immediately if you observe severe pain, swelling, rash, difficulty breathing, dizziness, or nausea and vomiting. Signs of a severe allergic reaction include swelling of the lips, face, or throat, difficulty breathing, rapid heartbeat, and severe dizziness. If any of these occur, take these actions: stop the procedure, notify the nurse immediately, follow emergency protocol, and call nine-one-one if the situation is life-threatening.

Documentation must happen immediately after each injection. Record the following on the MAR: the medication name, the dose administered, the route — whether SubQ or IM, the injection site used, the date and time, and any reactions observed. Accurate documentation ensures safe medication tracking, regulatory compliance, and continuity of care.

Let's review when to contact the nurse. Notify Real Care if a medication error occurs, if the individual refuses medication, if an adverse reaction occurs, if the injection site looks abnormal, or if there is a medication supply issue. Incident Reporting may be required. Prompt reporting protects the individual's health and safety.

As a delegated staff member, your responsibilities include following written instructions and injection protocol, maintaining infection control practices, respecting individual preferences, documenting accurately, and reporting concerns immediately. Injection administration cannot be delegated to untrained staff.

Finally, let's discuss competency demonstration. A Real Care nurse will verify your hand hygiene, medication preparation, correct injection technique, infection control practices, safe sharps disposal, proper documentation, and your ability to recognize adverse reactions. To complete this training, you must review the injection policy and protocol, demonstrate competency with a nurse, and sign the acknowledgment form. Competency is reassessed annually, when care plans change, and if incidents occur. Competency may also be verified via video observation.

Thank you for completing the Injection Administration training. If you have any questions, please contact Real Care staff.
