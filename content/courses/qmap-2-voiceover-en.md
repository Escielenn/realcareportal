QMAP Part 2 — Units 3, 4 & 5 — Voiceover Script (Full, Slide-by-Slide)

Welcome to Real Care! This is Part 2 of your Qualified Medication Administration Persons training. In this module, we'll walk through three units together: medication orders, the Medication Administration Record, and medication reminder boxes. By the end of this training, you'll know how to read and translate physician orders, document correctly on the MAR, and safely fill a medication reminder box for the clients you support.

Let's begin with Unit 3, which focuses on medication orders. Before you can administer any medication, you need to understand what a complete order looks like and what each part of that order is telling you.

Starting with Objective 1, let's go over the six required parts of a medication order. The first is the client's first and last name. The second is the medication itself. The third is the dose. The fourth is the route, meaning how the medication is given — by mouth, topically, and so on. The fifth is the date. And the sixth is the physician or provider's signature. If any of these six parts are missing, the order is incomplete, and you should not act on it.

Now for Objective 2, let's break down the dose itself. A dose actually has three parts. The first part is size — the amount of medication given at one time, like the number of tablets, milligrams, or milliliters. The second part is frequency — how often the medication is given, such as once daily, twice a day, every six hours, or as needed. The third part is duration — how long the medication should be taken, such as for seven days, ten days, or until the prescription is finished or the provider changes the order.

Moving to Objective 3, let's talk about strength of preparation. Strength of preparation refers to the concentration of medication in a specific form or amount. It tells you how much active drug is contained in each tablet, capsule, milliliter, or other dosage form. For example, a tablet labeled 500 milligrams means each tablet contains 500 milligrams of medication. Knowing the strength is what helps you make sure the correct dose is given.

For Objective 4, let's compare the metric system and the household system. In medicine, we use the metric system, and there's a good reason for that. The metric system is a decimal-based system of standard weights and measures using units like the milligram, gram, kilogram, milliliter, and liter. These units are precise and consistent everywhere they're used. One important thing to remember: a cubic centimeter is the same amount as a milliliter. One cc equals one mL.

The household system, on the other hand, is based on common measuring devices like teaspoons and tablespoons. The problem is that teaspoons and tablespoons can be different sizes from one kitchen to the next, which makes them unreliable for medication.

There are a few equivalents you'll need to learn. One teaspoon equals five cc, which equals five milliliters. Three teaspoons equal one tablespoon, which equals fifteen cc, which equals fifteen milliliters, which equals half an ounce. And two tablespoons equal thirty cc, which equals thirty milliliters, which equals one full ounce.

Now let's take a quick look at the measuring devices you'll be using. There are four common tools. The first is the medication cup, which is best for measuring liquid medications in larger volumes. The second is the measuring spoon, which is a calibrated spoon used for accurate teaspoon and tablespoon measurements. The third is the oral syringe, which is the most accurate tool and is especially useful for amounts less than five milliliters. And the fourth is the oral dropper, which is typically used for small doses, often with infants or for ear and eye medications. Always use the correct measuring tool for the dose you're giving, and don't substitute household teaspoons — they're not accurate.

Now for Objective 5, let's talk about your role as a QMAP in starting, changing, or stopping medication orders. First, you must have a written physician's order. Verbal and phone orders cannot be accepted by a QMAP. Faxed orders from a physician are acceptable, but faxes from a pharmacist are not — unless that fax is a copy of a signed physician order.

If a client returns from an inpatient hospital stay, the facility must obtain new orders from an authorized practitioner for every routine and PRN medication that was not included on the discharge orders. The phrase "resume previous orders" is not acceptable. Once you have a valid order, you'll properly document the new information on the MAR and follow Real Care, Inc. policies and procedures.

A couple of quick reminders about measurement: milligrams and milliliters are not the same thing. Always read the label carefully to make sure you're measuring the right one. And when measuring liquids, place the cup on a solid surface at eye level so you can read it accurately.

Now for Objective 6, let's go over common medical abbreviations you'll see on medication orders.

For timing: "ac" means before meals, "pc" means after meals, "bid" means twice a day, "tid" means three times a day, "qid" means four times a day, "HS" means hour of sleep or bedtime, "q" means every, "qd" means every day, "qh" means every hour, "q6h" means every six hours, "qod" means every other day, "prn" means as needed, and "DC" means discontinue.

For measurement: "mg" is milligram, "cc" is cubic centimeter, "mL" is milliliter, "Gm" is gram, "kg" is kilogram, "tsp" is teaspoon, "Tbsp" is tablespoon, "oz" is ounce, and "mEq" is milliequivalent.

For route and form: "po" means by mouth, "SL" means sublingual, "Buccal" means between the cheek and gum, "ophth" means ophthalmic, "otic" means ear, "OU" means both eyes, "OS" means left eye, "OD" means right eye, "tab" is tablet, "cap" is capsule, "EC" is enteric coated, "supp" is suppository, "sol" is solution, "oint" or "ung" is ointment, "gtts" is drops, and "XL" or "XR" means extended release.

And a few others: "c" with a line over it means with, "s" with a line over it means without, "x" means times, "R" in parentheses means right, and "L" in parentheses means left.

Now for Objective 7, let's practice translating some sample physician orders into plain language.

First example: Digoxin 0.125 milligrams, one tablet po qd. In everyday terms, this means Digoxin 0.125 milligrams, take one tablet by mouth once daily.

Second example: Coumadin 2.5 milligrams po hs on M, T, TH, F. This means Coumadin 2.5 milligrams, take by mouth at bedtime on Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday.

Third example: Coumadin 3 milligrams po hs on W, S, Su. This means Coumadin 3 milligrams, take by mouth at bedtime on Wednesday, Saturday, and Sunday.

Fourth example: Tylenol 325 milligrams, two tabs po q4 to 6h prn for knee pain, no more than 6 tablets per day. This translates to Tylenol 325 milligrams, take two tablets by mouth every four to six hours as needed for knee pain, not to exceed six tablets in one day.

Practicing these translations will help you read any physician's order with confidence.

Now we'll move into Unit 4, which covers the Medication Administration Record, also called the MAR. The MAR is one of the most important documents you'll work with as a QMAP, so let's go over how to use it correctly.

Starting with Objective 1, here are the nine essential rules for documenting medications. First, the MAR is a legal document, so your documentation must be accurate. Second, always document immediately after giving or monitoring a medication, never before. Third, document each administration or monitoring at the time it happens. Fourth, only document medications that you personally administered or monitored. Fifth, initial medications given or monitored in the box that matches the correct date and time. Sixth, always use black ink, never pencil. Seventh, never use white-out or attempt to erase an error. Eighth, remember the rule: if it's not documented, it wasn't administered or monitored. And ninth, there should be no blanks on the MAR.

Now for Objective 2, let's walk through some common documentation situations on the MAR. For discontinued medications, write the date and "DC" in large letters, draw a line through the rest of the date boxes, indicate that it's discontinued, and use a transparent yellow marker to highlight the name of the discontinued medication.

For new medications, transcribe them at the bottom of the list and draw a line through the dated boxes up to the start date. When you create a new MAR, always copy from the physician's orders — never copy from the old MAR sheet.

Remember that each medication must be documented at the time of administration. If you administer eight medications, you must initial the MAR eight separate times, indicating that each one was administered, refused, or unavailable. When a new order comes in, transcribe the new medication on the MAR. It's good practice to keep routine and PRN medications together on the MAR. And always follow your facility's policies and procedures regarding notification of new medications.

Now for Objective 3, let's go over what to do in some specific situations. If you make a charting documentation error, draw a single line through the mistaken entry, initial it and date it, and explain on the back of the MAR.

If a medication cannot be administered because it's not available or the client refuses it, circle the date box with your initials, document the exact reason on the reverse side or other designated area of the MAR, and contact the appropriate person according to Real Care, Inc. policy.

If you give a client the wrong dose of medication, report it to your supervisor immediately and follow Real Care, Inc. policies and procedures.

For a late entry, circle the date box with your initials, and you must document the situation in the notes section of the MAR.

Now for Objective 4, let's talk about how PRN documentation is different. First, initial the appropriate box on the MAR, then document on the reverse side — or the designated area — the time, dose, and the reason why the PRN medication was administered.

Then, check back with the client within thirty to sixty minutes and document whether they are better or worse on the reverse side of the MAR. Contact the appropriate person if necessary, and document that you have notified Real Care, Inc. if the client is not improved.

One important note: psychotropic medications cannot be given PRN, except in residential treatment facilities for the mentally ill, or in situations where the client understands the purpose of the medication and is capable of requesting it themselves.

Now we'll move into Unit 5, which covers medication reminder boxes. Let's go over what they are, who can fill them, and how to fill them safely.

Starting with Objective 1, let's define what a Medication Reminder Box, or MRB, actually is. An MRB is a compartmentalized container designed to hold medications for distribution by a time element such as day, week, or portions thereof. MRBs can be filled based on the client's self-medication assessment.

Now for Objective 2, let's talk about administration of medications from MRBs. Successfully completing this course allows you to fill MRBs under the supervision of a licensed professional or qualified manager. Regulations also allow medication reminder boxes used in designated settings to be filled by the client themselves, by a family member, or by a friend.

Now for Objective 3, let's go over the guidelines for filling MRBs. There must be a complete label firmly attached to the box. The label needs to include the client's name, the name of each medication, the dosage, the quantity, the route, and the specific time each medication is to be administered. If the design of the box doesn't allow firm attachment of the complete label, then the MRB cannot be used by a QMAP.

Certain medications must never be placed in an MRB. These include controlled substances, PRN medications, liquid medications, powders, inhalers, ointments and creams, and any medications with special instructions, such as "give thirty minutes prior to lunch."

A client who is self-administering medications may fill their own MRB and use it to store medications before taking them. Self-administered medications from an MRB must still be properly labeled, but they don't need to be documented on a MAR. However, there must be a MAR for recording all drugs placed in the MRB that are monitored or administered by staff.

Continuing with the guidelines for Objective 3, here are a few more important points. If there's a physician-ordered change to a client's medications, the client must stop using the MRB until the designated QMAP, nurse, or family member or friend has corrected the MRB according to the new order.

QMAPs must be familiar with the type and quantity of medication in each compartment of the box. If you suspect that the tablets or capsules in the MRB are not consistent with the label, do not proceed with administration until the problem is resolved. The QMAP who filled the MRB should resolve the difference.

A qualified medication manager must oversee a QMAP filling an MRB. The qualified manager should check the filling weekly during at least the first two times the MRBs are filled by a new QMAP, or by a QMAP who is a new employee, and periodically thereafter. A qualified manager must also be available for consultation whenever an MRB is being filled.

Now for Objective 4, let's walk through the steps for filling an MRB accurately and safely according to written physician orders. It's best practice to wear gloves when handling medications, especially if you'll be touching pills or clients.

Before you begin, fill the MRB in a safe, quiet, secured area, free from interruptions from staff, clients, and telephone calls. This helps avoid errors caused by distractions. Check all MRBs before filling for cleanliness and good repair. And fill the MRB for only one client at a time — this prevents mixing up medications between clients. Let's go through the specific steps.

Starting with Step 1: cross-check the MRB label with the physician's order, the MAR, and the pharmacist-prepared medication bottle. The label on the MRB should reflect the exact number of each tablet or capsule of medication to be placed in the MRB.

If the label on the MRB does not match the information on the physician's order, the MAR, or the medication bottle, you must resolve the discrepancy before filling the MRB. This includes verifying that the trade and generic names being used refer to the same drug.

Always ask for assistance when you're unsure of an order, a medication, a label, or the procedure used in filling MRBs. You are responsible for knowing your facility's policies and procedures for both filling MRBs and for administering or monitoring medications from them.

Moving on to Step 2: wash your hands immediately before opening any medication bottles, and then apply gloves. Transfer medications from the bottle lid into the MRB, or transfer them while wearing gloves — but never touch pills with bare hands. If you prefer, you can use clean tweezers to transfer medications from the bottle lid to the MRB. Alcohol wipes are acceptable for cleaning tweezers.

For Step 3: using an organized system, fill each medication on the MRB label one at a time, until all medications for that client are completed. Count the number of medications in the MRB and compare them to the MRB label to make sure they match.

And finally, Step 4: after filling is completed, count or estimate the number of pills remaining in the bottle. You should have enough medication in the bottle for at least ten days when possible, or as insurance allows. IRSS is responsible for reordering medications.

Now for Objective 7, let's review some important reference information that will help you support clients safely.

Your sources of information include medication labels and pharmacy instructions, physician or prescribing provider orders, the Medication Administration Record, your pharmacists, nurses, or healthcare providers, and approved drug reference guides.

Indications for use means the reason a medication is prescribed. It explains what condition or symptom the medication is intended to treat, manage, or prevent.

You should know the best time of day to administer each medication, because some medications work best when taken at specific times. Proper timing helps the medication work effectively, reduces side effects, and maintains the correct level of medication in the body.

You should also know common reactions and side effects. This helps you recognize what's normal and what may be a problem, so you can monitor clients for changes and report concerning symptoms to healthcare providers.

And finally, allergy considerations. Always check for known medication allergies before administering any medication. If a client has a known allergy, the medication should not be given, and the prescribing provider or nurse should be contacted immediately.
